# Synthesis Rules

Detailed rules for Step 1 of the uxr-report-building skill.

---

## Pseudonymization

- **Pseudonymize participants** with stable P-codes assigned in the manifest. This is not anonymization; combinations of context and quotes may still identify people.
- **Never** use real participant names or their employer's name in the report.
- **Product/feature names being studied are fine** — e.g., "the platform", "the checkout flow", "the prototype". The rule applies to *participants and their organizations*, not to the subject of the research.
- If a participant references a competitor or third-party tool by name, that's also fine to keep — it's signal.

---

## Quote Selection

A quote **illustrates** a theme — it is not the evidence for it. The evidence is the pattern across participants. State the prevalence before presenting the quote.

A good quote:
- **Shows the mechanism, not just the behavior** — why something happened, the reasoning or feeling behind it, not just the surface act.
- **Is specific and attributable** — clearly tied to one participant, contains enough context to be interpretable without paraphrasing.
- **Is representative, not the most dramatic** — vivid quotes create an availability heuristic risk. The best quote for a finding is the most representative one, not the most quotable.
- **Is 1–3 sentences** — trim filler ("um", "you know", "like") but never alter meaning.
- Use **ellipses (…)** for cuts within a quote.
- Use **[brackets]** for clarifying inserts (e.g., "[the approval screen]").

Attribute as: `— P3, Procurement Manager` (participant code + role only).

**Prohibited:** Quote-bombing — presenting multiple consecutive quotes without analytical commentary. Every quote must be followed by at least one sentence explaining what it demonstrates and how it connects to the theme.

---

## Convergence Thresholds

There is no universal numerical threshold for a strong qualitative finding. Report distinct participant counts and the relevant denominator, who was asked or observed, evidence quality, context and counter-cases. Distinguish not discussed from contradicted. Single-participant evidence can identify a consequential issue or warrant investigation, but emotion does not establish repetition or prevalence. Place important low-frequency findings where decisions require them and label the evidence honestly.

---

## Decision Rules: Make Them Explicit

For every theme in the synthesis output, document:
1. **How many participants contributed evidence** — e.g., "5 of 8 participants"
2. **What evidence criterion was applied** — observation, participant explanation, repeated mention, or other relevant basis
3. **What counter-evidence exists** — distinguish contradictory data from silence or absence of an opportunity to discuss the topic

This is not optional. Undocumented decision rules make findings unauditable. A reader must be able to assess whether conclusions were justified.

---

## Elite Bias Guard

Do not allow the most articulate, most verbose, or most frequent speakers to define themes for all participants. If one participant gave highly structured, quotable answers, their voice should not disproportionately shape theme construction. Check: would the theme still hold if the most articulate participant were removed?

---

## Implicit Answers

If a discussion-guide question went unanswered directly but was answered implicitly elsewhere in the transcript, capture it — and note in your synthesis that the answer was inferred, not stated.

---

## Outliers and Negative Cases

**Outliers are diagnostic, not noise.** Track findings that contradict main themes or come from a single participant with a distinctive context (different industry, role seniority, geography).

For every theme, actively search for data that contradicts or complicates it. For each negative case found, either:
- (a) Modify the theme to accommodate the counter-evidence, or
- (b) Report the contradiction explicitly with participant attribution.

Do not smooth contradictions into a single theme. Contradictions are data.
